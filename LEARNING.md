# Element Explorer — Learning Content (middle school)

**Audience:** middle school (≈ grades 6–8, NGSS MS‑PS1). Reading level kept simple.
**Goal:** prepare a student for a quiz on atomic structure — protons/neutrons/electrons,
mass and charge, isotopes, nuclear balance, and radioactive decay at a high level.
**Design:** each module is a short read → a *try it in the Element Explorer* interaction
→ a few check questions. The tool is the interactive backing for every concept.

This is the **content**, not the UI. Once it's approved we turn each module into a
"lesson card" and build a Quiz mode from the question bank at the bottom of each module.

> Scope chosen (adjust freely): the 8 core modules + **average atomic mass** + a light
> **ions→compounds** bridge, plus an optional **history of the atom** bonus.
> Answers are marked _A:_ under each question.

---

## 1. What's inside an atom?

**Goal:** name the three particles and where they live.

**The idea:** An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element. It has three parts:
- **Protons** and **neutrons** sit together in the **nucleus** — a tiny, dense clump at the center.
- **Electrons** zip around the outside in layers called **shells**.

The nucleus is incredibly small but holds almost all the atom's mass. The rest of the
atom is mostly empty space with electrons whizzing around.

**Key words:** atom, nucleus, proton, neutron, electron, electron shell.

**Common mistake:** thinking electrons are *inside* the nucleus. They're *outside*, in shells.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Add and remove each particle. Watch protons and neutrons land in
the center while electrons go onto the rings.

**Check yourself:**
1. Which two particles are found in the nucleus? _A: protons and neutrons._
2. Which particle moves around the outside of the atom? _A: the electron._
3. True/False: most of an atom is empty space. _A: True._

## 2. Charge — neutral atoms vs. ions

**Goal:** explain charge and how atoms become ions.

**The idea:** Each particle has a charge:
- proton = **+1**, electron = **−1**, neutron = **0** (no charge).

In a normal atom the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the pluses and
minuses cancel out — the atom is **neutral** (charge 0).

**Charge = protons − electrons.** If an atom **loses** electrons it has more pluses, so it
becomes **positive** (a **cation**). If it **gains** electrons it becomes **negative**
(an **anion**). An atom with a charge is called an **ion**.

Important: in chemistry, only **electrons** move in and out. Protons never leave.

**Key words:** charge, neutral, ion, cation (+), anion (−).

**Common mistake:** thinking you remove protons to make a positive ion. You remove *electrons*.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Keep protons the same and change the electrons. Watch the charge
readout and the Na⁺ / Cl⁻ style label update.

**Check yourself:**
1. An atom has 11 protons and 10 electrons. What is its charge? _A: +1._
2. An atom gains 2 electrons. Is it now a cation or an anion? _A: an anion (negative)._
3. What is an ion? _A: an atom with a charge (unequal protons and electrons)._
4. In a neutral atom, how do the number of protons and electrons compare? _A: they are equal._

## 3. Mass — how heavy is an atom?

**Goal:** know what gives an atom its mass and compute a mass number.

**The idea:** Protons and neutrons each weigh about **1 atomic mass unit (amu)**. Electrons
weigh almost nothing (about 1/1836 of a proton). So an atom's mass comes almost entirely
from the nucleus:

> **Mass number (A) = number of protons + number of neutrons.**

Electrons are so light we ignore them for mass.

**Key words:** atomic mass unit (amu), mass number (A).

**Common mistake:** counting electrons in the mass, or confusing mass number with atomic number.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Watch the "Mass number (A = Z + N)" readout change as you add
protons or neutrons — but notice it doesn't change when you add electrons.

**Check yourself:**
1. An atom has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. What is its mass number? _A: 14._
2. Why don't we count electrons when finding an atom's mass? _A: they weigh almost nothing._
3. Which particles give an atom almost all of its mass? _A: protons and neutrons._

## 4. Atomic number — the element's ID badge

**Goal:** connect proton count to element identity.

**The idea:** The **number of protons** is the **atomic number (Z)**, and it decides **which
element** the atom is. Change the number of protons and you change the element itself.
- Hydrogen always has 1 proton. Carbon always has 6. Oxygen always has 8.

The periodic table is arranged in order of atomic number. In a *neutral* atom, the number of
electrons also equals the atomic number.

**Key words:** atomic number (Z), element, chemical symbol.

**Common mistake:** thinking neutrons or electrons decide the element. Only **protons** do.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Drag the proton slider and watch the element name and symbol change.

**Check yourself:**
1. What determines which element an atom is? _A: the number of protons (atomic number)._
2. An atom has 8 protons. Which element is it? _A: oxygen._
3. If you added one proton to a carbon atom (6 protons), what would it become? _A: nitrogen (7 protons)._

## 5. Isotopes — same element, different neutrons

**Goal:** define isotopes and read isotope names.

**The idea:** Atoms of the **same element** always have the **same number of protons**, but
they can have **different numbers of neutrons**. These versions are called **isotopes**.
- They are still the same element (same chemistry) — they just have different masses.
- We name them element‑massnumber: **Carbon‑12**, **Carbon‑13**, **Carbon‑14**.

**Key words:** isotope.

**Common mistake:** thinking a different number of neutrons makes a different element. It doesn't —
it makes a different **isotope** of the same element.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Keep protons fixed and change the neutrons. The element stays the
same but the isotope name and mass number change. Check the isotope-abundance bars.

**Check yourself:**
1. Carbon‑12 and Carbon‑14 have the same number of what? _A: protons (and electrons)._
2. What is different between two isotopes of an element? _A: the number of neutrons._
3. Do isotopes of an element have the same atomic number? _A: Yes._
4. An atom of oxygen‑18 has 8 protons. How many neutrons does it have? _A: 10._

## 6. Average atomic mass — why the table's masses aren't whole numbers

**Goal:** explain the weighted-average mass on the periodic table.

**The idea:** The mass listed on the periodic table (like chlorine's **35.45**) is a
**weighted average** of all of that element's natural isotopes, weighted by how common each one is.
- Chlorine is about **75% Cl‑35** and **25% Cl‑37**, so the average lands near 35.5 — closer
  to the more common isotope.
- No single chlorine atom actually weighs 35.45; it's an *average* of a big mix.

**Key words:** average atomic mass, weighted average, abundance.

**Common mistake:** thinking the periodic-table mass is the mass of one atom. It's an average.

**Try it in the Explorer / Isotopes tool:** Change the mix of isotopes and watch the average
mass slide toward whichever isotope is more common.

**Check yourself:**
1. Why is chlorine's mass 35.45 instead of a whole number? _A: it's a weighted average of Cl‑35 and Cl‑37._
2. If an element were 90% of a light isotope and 10% of a heavy one, would the average be closer
   to the light or heavy isotope? _A: the light one (it's more common)._

## 7. A balanced nucleus — the proton/neutron tug-of-war

**Goal:** explain why atoms need a balance of neutrons and protons.

**The idea:** Protons are all positive, and like charges **push apart**, so the protons in a
nucleus are constantly trying to fly apart. **Neutrons act like glue** — they add a powerful
short-range pull (the **strong nuclear force**) *without* adding any extra push.
- Too few or too many neutrons and the nucleus becomes **unstable**.
- The stable atoms form a **band of stability**. Small atoms are stable with about **equal**
  protons and neutrons; big atoms need **more neutrons than protons** to hold together.

**Key words:** stable, unstable, band of stability, strong nuclear force.

**Common mistake:** thinking more neutrons always means more stable. There's a "just right" range.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Change neutrons and watch the badge switch between *occurs in nature*,
*lab-made*, *theoretical*, and *impossible*, and see the dot move on the neutron‑vs‑proton chart.

**Check yourself:**
1. What force holds the nucleus together against the protons pushing apart? _A: the strong nuclear force._
2. Why do neutrons help hold a nucleus together? _A: they add pull (glue) without adding positive charge/push._
3. Do heavy atoms need more or fewer neutrons than protons to be stable? _A: more neutrons._

## 8. Radioactive decay (high level)

**Goal:** describe what happens when an unstable nucleus decays.

**The idea:** An **unstable (radioactive)** nucleus doesn't last forever — it **decays**,
breaking down over time and releasing particles or energy (**radiation**) to become more stable.
Three simple types:
- **Alpha (α):** the nucleus spits out a chunk of **2 protons + 2 neutrons** (a helium nucleus).
- **Beta (β):** a **neutron turns into a proton** and shoots out an electron.
- **Gamma (γ):** the nucleus releases **energy** (no particles).

**Half-life** is the time it takes for **half** of the atoms in a sample to decay. It's random —
they don't all decay at once. Real examples: **Carbon‑14** is used to date old bones and wood;
**uranium** decays slowly over billions of years.

**Key words:** radioactive, radiation, decay, half-life, alpha/beta/gamma.

**Common mistake:** thinking a radioactive sample decays all at once, or instantly. Decay happens
gradually and randomly, measured by half-life.

**Try it in the Explorer:** Build unstable atoms (lab-made / theoretical) and note the "radioactive"
labeling; look up Carbon‑14 versus stable Carbon‑12.

**Check yourself:**
1. What is a half-life? _A: the time for half the atoms in a sample to decay._
2. In beta decay, a neutron turns into a ______. _A: a proton (plus an emitted electron)._
3. What does an unstable nucleus do over time? _A: it decays, giving off radiation to become more stable._
4. Which type of decay releases a particle made of 2 protons and 2 neutrons? _A: alpha decay._

## 9. From ions to compounds (bridge preview)

**Goal:** connect charge to why atoms stick together — a preview of bonding.

**The idea:** Atoms are "happiest" with a **full outer shell** of electrons (like the unreactive
noble gases). To get there:
- **Metals** tend to **lose** electrons → become **positive** ions.
- **Nonmetals** tend to **gain** electrons → become **negative** ions.

Then **opposite charges attract**, so the ions stick together and form a **compound**. Example:
sodium (Na⁺) + chlorine (Cl⁻) → **NaCl**, ordinary table salt. (The Bonding tool goes deeper.)

**Key words:** valence electrons, compound, chemical bond (preview).

**Check yourself:**
1. Do metals usually gain or lose electrons? _A: lose (become positive)._
2. Why do a positive ion and a negative ion stick together? _A: opposite charges attract._
3. What compound forms from Na⁺ and Cl⁻? _A: NaCl (table salt)._

## 10. Reading the periodic table (bridge preview)

**Goal:** read a table cell and understand rows vs. columns.

**The idea:** Each box shows the **symbol**, the **atomic number**, and the **average atomic mass**.
- A **row (period)** roughly tells you how many electron shells the atom has.
- A **column (group)** groups elements that **act alike**, because they have the **same number of
  outer (valence) electrons**.
- Metals are mostly on the left, nonmetals on the right, and the **noble gases** (far right column)
  barely react because their outer shell is already full.

**Key words:** period (row), group (column), valence electron.

**Check yourself:**
1. What do elements in the same column of the table share? _A: the same number of valence (outer) electrons; similar behavior._
2. What three things does a periodic-table box usually show? _A: symbol, atomic number, average atomic mass._
3. Why are the noble gases so unreactive? _A: their outer electron shell is already full._

## Bonus (optional): How our picture of the atom changed

**Goal:** a short "how we know" story often asked on quizzes.

- **Dalton (~1803):** atoms are tiny, solid, indivisible balls.
- **Thomson (~1897):** discovered **electrons**; pictured the atom as a "plum pudding" — electrons
  stuck in a ball of positive charge.
- **Rutherford (~1911):** the **gold foil experiment** showed the positive charge is packed into a
  tiny, dense **nucleus** at the center.
- **Bohr (~1913):** electrons travel in fixed **shells / energy levels** around the nucleus.
- **Modern:** electrons live in fuzzy **clouds** — we know where they *probably* are, not exact paths.

**Check yourself:**
1. Whose experiment discovered the tiny dense nucleus? _A: Rutherford (gold foil)._
2. Who discovered the electron? _A: Thomson._
3. Which model put electrons in fixed shells/energy levels? _A: Bohr's model._

## Quiz-mode blueprint (for later)

When we build the UI, the plan is:
- **Lesson cards**: the read + a "try it in the Explorer" prompt + the check questions with
  instant right/wrong feedback and a one-line explanation.
- **Quiz mode**: pulls a random mix from the question banks above; includes a few
  **"build this atom"** tasks that are graded by reading the Explorer's live state
  (e.g., "Build a neutral atom of oxygen‑18" → checks protons = 8, neutrons = 10, charge = 0).
- **Vocabulary list**: auto-collected from the **Key words** lines.
- Progress/score tracked in the browser (no accounts needed).

### Sample "build this atom" tasks
1. Build a **neutral carbon‑12** atom. _(6 p, 6 n, 6 e)_
2. Build a **sodium ion, Na⁺**. _(11 p, neutral-mass isotope, 10 e)_
3. Build an atom of **oxygen‑18**. _(8 p, 10 n)_
4. Build a **chloride ion, Cl‑37 as Cl⁻**. _(17 p, 20 n, 18 e)_
5. Make an **unstable/radioactive** version of carbon. _(e.g., 6 p, 8 n → Carbon‑14)_
